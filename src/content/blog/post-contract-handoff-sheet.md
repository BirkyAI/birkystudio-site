Every transaction has a seam in it, and it opens at the exact moment everybody relaxes. The contract is signed, the listing agent exhales, and the file changes hands. Nobody calls that a step. It has no owner and no deadline on any checklist, so it happens in whatever gap is left over, usually in a car with the phone on speaker.

A listing agent on r/RealEstateTechnology described the aftermath better than any software brochure I have read:

> *"Small misreads surface days later as a scramble, and by then nobody traces it back to the handoff."*

Nobody traces it back, and that is the expensive part. Two weeks later a date slips, the closing gets pushed, and nobody in the room can point at the moment the deal actually bent. It bent in the handoff.

## A contract is a summary, not a story

Read a purchase contract and you will find every outcome: price, dates, contingencies, who pays what. What you will not find is the story underneath it.

The story is that the buyer's lease ends on the 14th, so a two week delay is not an inconvenience for them, it is a mover and a storage unit and a very upset family. The story is that the seller called one contingency date "firm" in a tone that suggested it was anything but. The story is that the buyer asked, on the driveway, whether the washer and dryer stay, and somebody said yes without writing it down anywhere.

None of that is in the contract. All of it decides whether the file closes cleanly or turns into three weeks of phone calls.

## The step nobody owns

Look at a transaction and every stage has a name attached. The listing agent owns the listing. The lender owns the appraisal and the loan. The inspector owns the report. Title owns the search. Somebody owns every single line, and every line has a date.

Then there is the handoff. Ask who owns it and the honest answer is that everybody assumes somebody else does. That is not a discipline problem, it is an ownership problem, and it looks exactly like the inbound lead problem we wrote about in [who hooks new leads up to searches](https://birkystudio.com/blog/inbound-leads-die-without-mls-alerts/): a step that nobody was assigned, per file, forever, so it happens late or not at all.

The fix in both cases is the same. Stop treating it as a conversation that happened, and start treating it as a document that gets produced.

## The Handoff Sheet

One page, seven fields, written while the deal is still fresh. Here is the whole thing.

**1. The file, in one line.** Buyer, seller, property, and one reference number that everybody uses. Not four versions of the address across four systems.

**2. The dates that cannot move, and the dates that can.** Two short lists, with the name of whoever confirmed it. "Inspection objection, firm, buyer's agent confirmed in writing. Appraisal, soft, lender said the 11th but hedged." The single most expensive sentence in transaction coordination is "I assumed that one was firm."

**3. What was said out loud.** Every commitment that is not in the contract: the fence stays, the repair gets done before closing, the buyer gets a walkthrough before the movers arrive. If it was promised verbally, it goes here, plus who promised it.

**4. The pressure that is not written anywhere.** The lease ending, the job starting, the rate lock expiring, the school enrollment deadline, the relative flying in. This is the field that explains every future urgency, and the one that never survives a handoff because it sounds like small talk at the time.

**5. Open questions, each with a name next to it.** Not a worry list, an assignment. Every unresolved question gets a person who owes an answer, so the next call is "you owe me the 1099 figure", not "has anybody heard anything".

**6. What changed since the last version.** A deal file is a living thing. The coordinator does not need the whole history re-told, they need the delta: what moved, what slipped, what got resolved since Tuesday.

**7. How to reach the humans today.** Two numbers per side and the hours they actually answer. Not the office line that goes to a shared mailbox nobody opens.

That is the entire document. It reads in about a minute. Filled in for a real file it looks like this:

- **File:** buyer and seller, one property, one reference number
- **Firm:** inspection objection, written confirmation attached
- **Soft:** appraisal window, lender hedged by two days
- **Said out loud:** fence stays, garage shelving goes with the seller
- **Pressure:** buyer's lease ends mid month, no flexibility
- **Open:** HOA transfer fee amount, owned by listing side today
- **Changed:** sewer scope moved from Thursday to Monday
- **Reach:** both sides have a mobile number and an evening window

Seven fields on one page, and it costs perhaps fifteen minutes to write. Those fifteen minutes are the difference between a coordinator who starts work tomorrow and a coordinator who spends tomorrow interviewing three people to find out what already happened.

## Why the page does not already exist

Not because agents are careless. Because the moment a deal goes under contract is the moment the agent's calendar fills up, and because writing up a handoff is nobody's job, and because every system in the stack assumes the structured data arrives complete on its own. It never does. It arrives in sentences, half of them in someone's head.

I have never once heard a real estate agent say the problem was the contract. Every version of this story I have heard ends in the same place: the details existed, and then they did not.

## What this will not do

- It cannot attend the conversation in the driveway. A detail that was never said into a channel it handles does not exist to it, and no honest vendor will tell you otherwise.
- It cannot read intent out of a contract. It can record who said a date was firm and flag the contradiction. Judging which dates really are immovable is a human call, and it should stay one.
- It cannot decide who owns an open question. You assign those.
- It will not quote you a "we save X days per closing" number. Your files, your market and your tolerances decide that, and I did not measure them.

What it removes is the class of failure nobody traces back: the detail that was known, verbal, and then gone.
