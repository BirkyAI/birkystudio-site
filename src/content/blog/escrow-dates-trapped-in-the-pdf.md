There is a post from last week in r/RealEstateTechnology that is not really a complaint. A developer announced a free beta for a tool that uploads a purchase agreement, pulls out the important details, and turns them into a timeline that the agent and the client can both follow. The line they used is the interesting part:

> *"A tool to stop the constant 'any update?' texts."*

Thirty agents turned up for the beta with a hundred and fifty contracts between them. Nobody in that thread argued about rates or inventory. They signed up because their clients keep asking the same question and they are tired of answering it.

I want to be straight about what that thread proves. It does not prove the dates are missing. Every one of those contracts has the dates in it, typed in correctly, on page four. It proves that a PDF is not a place anybody can look.

## The dates are already written down

Open any purchase agreement and you will find them: the inspection window, the appraisal, the financing contingency, the walkthrough, the closing. Five dates that set the rhythm of the escrow, all of them correct, all of them locked inside a document that lives on one person's laptop.

So the record already exists. What does not exist is the other half of it: a place where those dates are visible to the person who is waiting on them.

That is the entire gap. Not capture. Visibility.

## Thirty-one days, nine questions

```
Day 3,  11:26am  You:    Inspection booked for Thursday, on track.

```

Read the messages on the left, then read the replies. Every single answer in that log came out of opening the purchase agreement again. Not reading it for pleasure: finding the date, checking the addendum, confirming the number before typing a reply. The truth lives in a document, so answering a two-line question means going back into the document.

Now read the shape of it. The client wrote nine messages in the first thirty-one days of the contract. Six of them carried no new information at all. They were not asking a question so much as checking whether anyone was still holding the file. That is what a client does when the only instrument they have for finding out where things stand is you.

Count the look-ups if you want. Nine questions, nine trips into the document. If each trip is four minutes, that is thirty-six minutes of document archaeology in one deal, and I am not going to convert that into a dollar figure, because I made the log up and you should put your own minutes in.

## The dates that move are the ones that land nowhere

Here is the part that matters more than the arithmetic. The five dates on page four are the ones that rarely surprise anybody. They were written down at signature.

The dates that actually move arrive differently. The inspector can do Thursday instead of Tuesday. The appraiser wants to come back. Title needs one more signature before it releases. The lender's underwriter wants a letter about a deposit that showed up in August. None of that is on page four. It arrives as a sentence, in the middle of a call about something else, and it changes the story the client is holding.

That is the same problem as the [note you were never going to write](/blog/phone-note-chaos-transaction-calls/), one level up. The detail gets said out loud, and the only copy of it is in the head of the person who heard it.

## The step nobody owns

Nobody owns the update. That is the honest reason it goes out late, or in one lump at the end, or from a phone at 9pm with a bad typo.

Name the roles on one deal: agent, transaction coordinator, lender, title officer, inspector, appraiser, and a buyer with a moving truck booked. Not one of those job descriptions contains the sentence "tells the client where things stand on a Tuesday."

So it falls to whoever remembers, which in practice means the agent, which means it happens after the showings and the calls and the two other deals in flight, which means it happens three days late and only because the client asked first.

It is the same shape of problem as [inbound leads that never get hooked up to a search](/blog/inbound-leads-die-without-mls-alerts/): a step that has to happen once per person, per deal, forever, and has no owner, no deadline, and no system. In a healthy deal you get away with it. On the deal with the surprise underwriting letter, the update is the thing that stops a manageable delay from becoming a phone call to your broker.

## What this cannot do

- **It cannot read a contract nobody uploaded.** If the agreement stays in a downloads folder, the dates stay in the PDF. That is a one-time step at signature, and it is on you.
- **It cannot judge.** Whether a date is firm or flexible, whether to push the seller on a repair credit, what to tell a buyer who is about to lose their rate lock: those are your calls, and they are the reason you have the license.
- **It will not tell a client something it cannot point at.** If a date has not been confirmed in writing, it does not get presented as confirmed. Slightly less polished updates, considerably fewer retractions.
- **I will not quote you a number for the questions it prevents.** The log above is a composite and I am not going to turn my own made up example into a statistic about your clients.
