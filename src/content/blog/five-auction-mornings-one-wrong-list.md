## The list is official. That is the problem.

On 16 October an investor posted this to r/RealEstateTechnology:

> "My county publishes upcoming foreclosure sales but the list isn't always accurate. Sometimes I'll show up to auctions and they will announce sales that weren't included on their published calendar."

Read it again, because the word doing the damage is published. He is not describing a scam or a broken website. He is describing a document his county produces on purpose, that looks authoritative, that he plans a week around, and that starts going out of date the moment it is printed.

The calendar is not wrong because somebody is careless. It is wrong because it is a document, and the room is not.

## Five mornings

*The log below is a composite, built from auction weeks I have watched and compared notes on. The dates move around. The pattern does not.*

```
Auction mornings, October 2026

6 Oct    sheet: 3 sales    in the room: 5, two never listed
         3.5 hours         two sales you could never plan for

8 Oct    sheet: 2 sales    both postponed, announced at 9:10am
         2.5 hours         drove for nothing

14 Oct   sheet: 1 sale     canceled the week before, sheet unchanged
         2.0 hours         the clerk knew, the paper did not

21 Oct   sheet: 4 sales    one withdrawn, three went ahead
         4.0 hours         one below reserve

28 Oct   sheet: 2 sales    both went ahead as printed
         3.0 hours         the paper and the room agreed
```

Twelve dated rows. Four of them described sales that never happened. Two sales happened that were never on the sheet at all. Fifteen hours of mornings, and only one of those mornings produced something worth bidding on.

Notice which row was the good one. It was 28 October, the boring morning where the paper and the room agreed. You cannot tell that from the sheet in advance. That is the whole problem.

## Why the paper cannot be current

**It is compiled, then printed.** The list comes from filings gathered up to a cutoff and formatted into a calendar. Anything filed after the cutoff, or changed after it, lands in the room instead of on the page.

**Postponements and withdrawals are administrative.** When a sale is pulled, the people who need to know are the ones standing in the room. Nobody re-issues a published calendar for a postponement, so the canceled sale keeps sitting on the sheet, looking exactly like the real ones.

**Three documents describe one sale and none of them is the authority.** The calendar is a summary. The register of actions holds the status. The notice or affidavit holds the trustee, the confirmation requirement and the terms. They are written by different offices, updated on different clocks, and they disagree more often than anyone outside the process expects. The same property can also arrive under a new case number after a re-filing, which is why two people searching the same house can come back with two different answers ([Duplicate Property Records Cost You Deals](https://birkystudio.com/blog/duplicate-property-records-cost-you-deals/) is the same failure in a different part of the stack).

I wrote the reconciliation side of this in October and it is still the shortest version of the rule: the county list is a starting point, not the truth ([The Published List Is Not the Truth](https://birkystudio.com/blog/published-list-is-not-the-truth/)).

## The three checks nobody runs

For every sale on the sheet, before you commit a morning to it:

1. Pull the docket entry and read the most recent filing date, not the hearing date.
2. Open the notice itself and confirm the sale is still scheduled, still confirmed, and still with the same trustee or referee.
3. Phone the clerk or the trustee's office and ask one question: has anything been filed on this case in the last 72 hours? Postponements almost never arrive earlier than that.

Four minutes per sale. Twelve sales means most of a morning, which is the same fifteen hours you were trying to save, spent at a desk instead of in a car. So the checks do not get run. Not because anyone is lazy, but because the person doing them is also the person driving, bidding, and answering the phone.

That is the honest failure here. The information exists, it is public, and there is nobody whose job is to read it.

## What it will not do

- It will not tell you what a property will sell for. It tells you whether the sale is still real, and when it last changed.
- It will not turn a bad lot into a good one. Most of the sales on that sheet are not opportunities, and the agent is not going to pretend otherwise.
- It cannot see a postponement announced in the room after the clerk's desk closes for the day.
- It will not hide a disagreement between sources. If the docket and the notice disagree, you get both and a flag, because that disagreement is usually the sale you should look at hardest.
