> "Longer listing appointments to compensate"

An agent posted that line in r/RealEstateTechnology and moved straight on to the next paragraph, which is why it is worth stopping on. It means the appointment runs long because the agent cannot recall the last conversation, so the hour gets padded: questions asked twice, stories retold, and nobody sure whether the seller noticed.

The padding is not the bill. The bill arrives eighteen months later.

## The Tuesday that ends with not yet

*The appointment below is a composite. The words are illustrative. The pattern is not.*

It is a bright Tuesday afternoon. The house is warm and full of low autumn light, the seller has done their homework, and they like you. Then, at the kitchen table, they say the sentence every agent knows:

```
"We want to redo the kitchen first. Maybe next spring."
```

You leave with a good feeling, a compliment, and no listing. Eighteen months later a sign goes up on that house. Sometimes it is your sign. Very often it is not.

## What actually died in those months

Nobody hung up on you. Nobody said no. What died was the only thing that would have brought you back at the right moment: what that seller told you in the kitchen.

They told you what had to be true before they would list. They told you where they would go if it sold in the spring. They even told you how often they wanted to hear from you. Three sentences, and by Wednesday morning they are gone, because your pipeline has a stage called Active and a stage called Closed and nothing in between called Later.

An agent on the same forum described the daily version of this better than any feature list could:

> "Less 'smart,' more 'impossible to forget to log.'"

That is the whole complaint about a CRM, in one line. Years of features got built, and what agents actually needed was somewhere a sentence lands without effort, plus a next step attached to it. A house that is not for sale yet has no stage, so the sentence lands nowhere at all, and a note with no deadline does not get written.

## The question that gets you nowhere

Almost every agent ends that appointment the same way:

```
"So when do you think you would be ready?"
```

It is a fair question and it produces a polite non-answer, because the honest answer is "we do not know yet". You have just been granted permission to follow up exactly zero times.

Three questions do better, and they only help if the answers land somewhere you will read again.

```
"What has to be true before you would list?"
```

The kitchen. The school year. The mother-in-law moving out in March. That answer is a date in disguise, and it is also the reason a market update in March reads as useful instead of noisy.

```
"If it sold in the spring, where would you go?"
```

This is the one that separates a warm seller from a real one. If the answer is "we would have to buy something", you are looking at two deals in one kitchen instead of one.

```
"How often do you want to hear from me?"
```

Almost nobody asks this. Ask it and you get the seller's own words on how often they expect to hear from you. That is consent, it is specific, and it is the asset. It also expires, and it expires fastest with the sellers who said not yet.

## The record is the product

Here is the part that has nothing to do with AI. Those three answers disappear because no pipeline was ever built for a house that is not for sale, so writing the sentence down has nowhere to go, and nothing in the system will ever ask you for it again. I have written about the same gap from the client's side in [Clients Remember What You Forget](https://birkystudio.com/blog/clients-remember-what-you-forget/), and the version that costs money is this one: it is not the buyer who notices, it is the seller who quietly lists with somebody else.

Which is why the fix is not another CRM to evaluate. It is one running note per house, four lines long:

- What has to be true before they list.
- Where they would go if it sold.
- How often they want to hear from you.
- The date you next speak.

That is the record. Everything else in this post is about what happens to those four lines after you drive away.
