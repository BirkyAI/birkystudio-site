Not one step in that sequence was a lie, and that is exactly what makes it expensive. Every single one was sold as help. The bill just moved to somewhere she could not see it.

## Content marketing does not fail on ideas

I have watched a lot of small businesses try this, mine included. The failure is almost never "we could not think of anything to write". It is "we published nine things in January and nothing in June".

So before the money, the arithmetic. **Four hours a week is 208 hours a year.** That is not an industry statistic, it is multiplication, and you can put your own number in. Three hours a week is 156 hours. Eight hours is 416. Anybody who hands you a real "industry average" for content hours should be asked where they measured it, because I have never seen one that survives the question.

*What follows is a composite. The details are illustrative and the pattern is one I have watched across agencies, agents and small businesses, more than once.*

## The first bill: your own week

- The article nobody assigned you, which still needs an idea, an opening line, and facts that hold up when a client checks.
- The Google Business Profile, which has no deadline, so it happens in bursts and then goes quiet for two months.
- The follow-up email to your own list, the only audience that comes with the relationship attached.
- The reply to a client asking a question you have already answered nine times. That is content too, and it is the same question the next person is about to type into Google.
- The description of the thing you are selling, written at 9pm from four photographs.

Call it five pieces a week. Five pieces a week is **260 a year**. That is the commitment being made when somebody tells you to "just be consistent".

## The second bill: the programs you bought to escape the first bill

This is where the treadmill starts, and it does not feel like a treadmill because every step is small: a scheduler, a template pack, a stock library, a course. Then the course tells you that you should not be doing this yourself, so you add a monthly editing or coaching program.

None of them is expensive on its own. All of them are subscriptions. And the quiet trap underneath is that none of them writes anything. You are still the one who has to open the laptop, and the charge arrives whether you did or not.

There is a second version of the same trap, and it is the one I see in real estate agencies constantly: buying tools that publish to social platforms, then discovering that the platform's own algorithm decides how many people see it, forever. The one place your content keeps working without a monthly argument is your own site and your Google profile.

## The third bill: the silence, which nobody invoices

This is the bill I would put in front of every owner, because it has no line item anywhere.

When the publishing stopped: your site has not changed since 2024. Your Google profile looks like a business that has closed. The person searching for exactly what you do, in your city, found somebody who published last week. You cannot see any of that on a bank statement, so it never registers as a cost. It reads as "we have been busy".

Nothing on that list is dramatic, which is the problem. There is no email that arrives to tell you it happened.

**The setup is work.** Consistency is not a personality trait, it is a system, and a system has to be configured before it runs: what gets published, where, in which language, on which days, in whose voice, and what happens when you disagree with a draft. Every demo skips this part, because the demo is the fun part.
