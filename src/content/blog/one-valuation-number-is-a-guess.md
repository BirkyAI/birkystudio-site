An agent on Reddit put the whole problem in one line: the 2 to 3 percent accuracy everyone advertises only holds up on standard properties. In low transaction ZIP codes, the confidence interval widens past 15 percent, and most tools do not show you that difference. They show you a clean number either way.

That is the sentence I want to sit in the middle of this post, because we sell AI and this is the part of AI we refuse to oversell.

## The number that looks the same on every house

Here is the same valuation tool, run on three different houses, printed the way it prints:

```
Standard home, active subdivision, recent comps     $412,000    expected error 2.5%
Custom home, 40 years old, one of a kind            $418,000    expected error 8%
Rural property, low transaction ZIP                 $405,000    expected error 16%
```

Look at the shape of those three lines. Same font. Same currency sign. Same three digits, sitting there with the same quiet confidence. If I handed you only the numbers and nothing else, you would read all three the same way, as a fact.

Only one of them is close to a fact. The first house sits in a neighborhood where twelve similar homes sold in the last ninety days. The tool has seen a hundred versions of it. The third house sits on a road where four homes sold in a year, and none of them looked like this one. The tool has almost nothing, so it does what every model does when it has almost nothing: it guesses, and it prints the guess in the exact same typeface.

A person reads the middle column as one number. Software reads it as one number. Neither one flinches. That is the whole trap.

## What a flat number costs you

Walk the second house through a real sale.

You sit with the seller. The tool said $418,000, so you say $418,000, and you say it in your holding-a-number voice, the one clients trust. You list at $418,000. Six weeks later the appraisal comes back at $372,000, because the appraiser actually walked the house and found the things the model could not see, the layout, the lot, the condition.

The deal stalls. The seller is not angry at the tool. The seller has never heard of the tool. The seller is angry at you, because you were the one who said the number out loud.

Nothing on that page was a lie. $418,000 was the model's honest output. The lie was the missing eighth word, the one that should have come right after it: roughly. One flat number, delivered with total confidence, and you spent your credibility buying back the difference.

## The tool you trust is not really yours

There is a second half to this that agents know in their bones.

An agent on Reddit, furious about ATTOM's new property navigator, wrote it plainly: they took a simple, easy-to-use interface and turned it into a mess, and the comparative market analyses look worse than they did before. Same vendor, same subscription, nothing they could do about it. The tool that held their number and their workflow got redesigned on a Tuesday and the redesign was not theirs to approve.

So think about what you are actually depending on. A number you cannot see the range of, behind an interface a vendor can rebuild without asking you, priced on a plan they can change next quarter. When all three of those are true at once, the polish of the screen stops mattering. What matters is whether the number comes with its uncertainty attached, whether it keeps the source it drew from, and whether it stays legible when the tool vendored underneath it shifts.

That is the difference between a valuation you can show your work with and a valuation you can only hope about.

## What showing your work actually looks like

The version of this we build runs the other way. Every estimate carries a visible range, not a single number. When the model is confident because it has a hundred nearby comps, you see a tight band and you can trust it. When it is guessing because the road is quiet, the band widens on the screen and the tool says so in plain words, and that wide band is not the tool failing. It is the tool telling you the truth about how much it knows.

From there it does three things a flat number cannot:

- It keeps the sources behind the estimate, so when a seller asks where the number came from, you can open the file instead of shrugging.
- It flags the moment the band gets wide enough that a human appraiser belongs in the conversation, and it says that out loud rather than letting you discover it at the appraisal.
- It remembers what was said in the conversation around the number, so the seller's own words about the price are still sitting in the record when the appraisal lands six weeks later and everybody is looking for someone to blame.

## What we will not pretend

- We will not tell you our valuations are accurate to a single percent. Ask any vendor who claims that which market they measured, on which houses, and watch the number move.
- The agent cannot see the inside of a house it was never shown, cannot know the kitchen was redone last spring unless someone told it, and cannot turn a quiet road into a busy one.
- A wide confidence band is not something to sell you a fix for. Sometimes the honest answer is that this house needs a walk through and a human opinion, and that is what the tool should say.
- We will not hide the range to make the product feel more impressive. A number that looks certain and isn't is worse than no number at all.
