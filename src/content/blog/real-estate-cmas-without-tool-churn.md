An agent on r/RealEstateTechnology put it better than any marketing team could:

> *"Anyone else absolutely despise ATTOM's new property navigator and the way the comparative market analyses look? They took a simple, easy to use GUI and made a mess out of it."*

That's pain point #18 in the running thread I keep coming back to — and it's the quietest one in the list. Nobody loses a deal the day a tool redesign ships. There's no "I lost a client because the buttons moved." But read between the lines and you can hear exactly what happened: an agent who had a **working CMA workflow**, built over months, woke up one morning and had to learn it again. For free. On their own time.

It's the same complaint that sits underneath pain #10 — "I can't get good data sources for property analysis without switching between three or four tools." The tools were already a shuffle. Now one of the tools in the shuffle got worse, and there's no downgrade button.

I don't think agents should have to be hostages to that. So here's what I'd tell any agent before they trust a new CMA tool with their listings.

## The real problem isn't the redesign

Let's be fair to the vendors. Every piece of software changes. A redesign isn't automatically a betrayal.

The problem is **what you depend on**. If the number a family makes a six-figure decision with lives inside somebody else's interface — an interface that can change without asking you — then your business model has a single point of failure that isn't yours.

The redesign just made that visible. The dependency was always there.

So the question isn't "which CMA tool is best this month." That's the trap. Tools change. Rankings change. The question is: **what do you actually need from whatever you use?**

Here are the three things I'd demand. If a tool can't do all three, it isn't ready to sit under your livelihood.

## Non-negotiable 1: It tells you how sure it is

Most CMA tools hand you a number like it's a fact. *Property's worth $412,000.* Clean, confident, single.

But there's no single number in this business. The agent who flagged this earlier this year on the same forum said it well: a valuation that runs "within 2-3%" only holds on standard properties in active metro ZIPs — out in a low-transaction pocket, the real confidence range can balloon past 15%. Show one flat number to a seller and you've borrowed trust you might have to pay back at the worst possible moment.

So the first thing I want from any tool I lean on: **it shows me the range, not just the point.** A tight band means I can speak with conviction. A wide one means I should be reaching for an appraiser, not a slideshow. Knowing the difference is the job. We wrote a whole post about why we build it that way — [you can read it here](/blog/ai-valuation-confidence-intervals/).

If your current tool never shows you a confidence range, it is hiding a decision from you.

## Non-negotiable 2: It pulls the comps itself

The second thing — and this is where the "three or four tools" pain actually bites — is that the analysis shouldn't start with a scavenger hunt.

If I have to open the MLS, then a paid analytics product, then a spreadsheet, then my email for the last comparable that an agent forwarded me last month, I'm not doing analysis. I'm doing data entry with extra steps. Every one of those hops is a place where the number can get stale, mis-keyed, or just not finished before the seller's patience runs out.

A tool should pull recent sales, active listings, and neighborhood trends into one place, from live data, when you ask. One question in, one defensible answer out. I broke down the true cost of the four-tab shuffle in [The Real Cost of One Comps Check](/blog/market-data-comps-on-demand/) — the number is higher than most agents guess.

## Non-negotiable 3: The output is yours

This is the one the ATTOM thread is really about.

Whatever you build — the CMA, the comparison set, the client-facing summary — should be **portable**. Exportable. Yours to keep, print, send, or file, independent of whether the vendor redesigns their interface next quarter.

If the only place your analysis exists is inside a screen somebody else controls, then you don't own your work. You're renting it, and the rent can go up, or the layout can change, without your vote.

The AI agent we build lives on top of the tools you already have — it doesn't ask you to abandon your MLS or your CRM for Yet Another Platform. You ask a question in plain language (WhatsApp, text, wherever you already are), it pulls the relevant data, and it hands back something you can actually **send to a client** — a comparable set, a price range, a one-paragraph summary of why. It writes it into your notes too, so the context doesn't disappear after you close the app.
