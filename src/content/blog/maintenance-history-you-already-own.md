A small landlord posted this on Reddit a few weeks ago, in a thread where people were comparing apps for keeping track of rentals:

> "I feel like I have very little visibility into my rentals other than knowing how much I spent each year and it feels like im losing money somewhere. I have invoices, repair records, etc organized in my google drive but it's annoying trying to look through them all. I don't really have a good way to track the age and condition of major things like furnaces, water heaters, roofs, etc. I think I would want to know what might need replacing soon."

Read that twice, because the second reading is the interesting one.

He is not saying he lost the paperwork. He is not saying he is disorganized. He says the records are there, organized, in a folder he can open any time. What he cannot do is ask them anything.

That is a much smaller problem than it sounds, and it is also a much more common one than people admit. I hear a version of it from almost every self-managing landlord I talk to: the documents exist, and the knowledge does not.

## What a folder actually stores

*The folder below is a composite of how self-managing owners describe their records to me: the real file names, the real habits, one property. The numbers are the shape of a real example, not a quote from one client.*

```
Dupont St (2 units, five years of records)
  Receipts 2019 to 2024/          20 files
  Invoices/                       63 files
  Insurance/                       4 files
  Invoice.pdf
  invoice.pdf
  invoice (1).pdf
  Invoice FINAL.pdf
  screenshot-furnace-sticker.jpg
  IMG_2841.jpg
  IMG_2841 (1).jpg
  water heater email (forwarded).msg
  roof quote (do not use).pdf
  Tax return 2021.pdf
```

Five years of records. A folder that opens. And not one line in it that is a fact about anything.

Now sit that folder down and ask it the five questions an owner actually has. Print the answers exactly as they come back:

```
Q: How old is the water heater in unit 2?
A: unknown. There is a photo of a sticker taken in 2021.

Q: When was the roof last done?
A: unknown. There is a quote marked "do not use" from 2022.

Q: What did I spend on the Dupont property last year?
A: about $2,410, assuming every receipt made it into the folder.

Q: What is likely to need money in the next twelve months?
A: unknown.

Q: Is the furnace still under any warranty?
A: unknown.
```

One of those five answers is a number. The other four are the same word.

That is the whole problem, and it is worth saying plainly: **a folder stores documents, it does not store facts.** A fact about an asset is small and specific. It has an age, a condition, a last service date, an expected life and a next action. A PDF invoice has none of those things. It is a picture of a transaction, and the transaction is the least useful part of it. The useful part was what the person doing the work said while they were standing there, which is the part that never made it into any file at all.

If that sounds familiar, it is the same thing that happens to transaction calls, which I wrote about in [The Note You Didn't Write Is Costing Deals](https://birkystudio.com/blog/phone-note-chaos-transaction-calls/): the detail gets said out loud, everyone agrees it matters, and nobody writes it down.

## Why this stays invisible until it is expensive

Nothing about an empty register hurts on a Tuesday. That is exactly why it survives for years.

The cost lands in one week, all at once, and it rarely arrives as the repair bill itself. Ask anyone who has replaced a furnace in February on an emergency call. The money is not the only thing that leaves. It is the tenant without heat for two days, the second unit whose heater was installed the same year as the first one, the unplanned week you spent on it, and the fact that you took the first contractor who could come rather than the one you wanted.

None of that is a failure of discipline. It is what a system with no forward view does: it meets everything for the first time. And the owner in that Reddit thread put his finger on the real symptom, which is not a bill. It is the sentence "it feels like im losing money somewhere." He is right, and the folder cannot tell him where.

## The register is small, which is why nobody builds one

A per-property asset register, in its useful form, is six columns and one line per thing that can break:

what it is, what unit it is in, when it was installed, what condition it is in now, how long it is likely to last, and what the next action is.

That is it. Furnace, water heater, roof, gutters, driveway, water main, the appliances you supplied. Eleven or twelve lines for a two-unit property, and most of them take a minute each. It is not a database project. It is a page, and the only reason it does not exist in most rentals is that the information needed to fill it in is scattered across five years of folders and a handful of conversations.

So we build it, and we keep it, because a register that depends on you remembering to update it will be a blank page by March. That part is honest and unglamorous: the useful thing is not the page, it is the habit of the page being written for you.

## What I will not tell you

Every promise here is small on purpose, because the honest version of this work is small:

- **It cannot see inside a unit nobody has written a line about.** A blank register stays honest about being blank, and the first thing it will do is ask you what you know.
- **It will not guess an age.** If the install year is unknown, the register says unknown and asks. A made up date on a furnace is worse than no date, because you would plan around it.
- **It cannot inspect, and it does not replace a contractor's eyes.** It organizes what the people who can see it tell you.
- **It will not stop things breaking.** Water heaters fail. It just stops every failure arriving as a complete surprise.
- **It will not quote you a savings figure.** I have seen numbers like "an asset register saves owners 30%" and they are guesses wearing a percentage sign. What I can tell you is which appliances in your two units are closest to the end, and that is enough to plan with.
