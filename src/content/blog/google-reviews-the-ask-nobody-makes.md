> "I have lead inquiries that come in all day. I rely on our agents to hook them up for MLS alerts via our IDX -- but unfortunately barely anyone does it."

That is a brokerage owner on r/RealEstateTechnology, and the word doing the work in that sentence is *barely*. Setting up an alert takes about thirty seconds and puts a live search in front of a lead, which is why nobody argues that the step is worth doing. It still does not happen, because it belongs to nobody and has no deadline attached to it.

## The review ask is the same step in a different room

Ask any business owner whether they should be asking past customers for a Google review and you get a nod. Ask when they last asked and you get the same answer every time: not recently, or we keep meaning to.

That is not a belief problem and it is not a laziness problem. The ask lives in the ten minutes between finishing one job and starting the next one, and those ten minutes are already spoken for. So the customers who would happily write three honest sentences about you never get asked, and the ones who had something to say say it to a friend instead.

I wrote separately about why the reviews you do have [read like they were written by strangers](https://birkystudio.com/blog/client-reviews-read-like-strangers/). Today I want to be concrete about the other half: what we actually do about it, in order, and the parts we refuse to do.

## Two halves, and almost nobody sells both

There are two halves to getting more reviews, and they get sold as one.

The first half is the ask. Finding the past customers, asking each of them properly, and doing it consistently enough that reviews are still arriving next month too. This is the half with no owner inside your business.

The second half is staying inside Google's rules, and this is the half that quietly costs businesses their profile. Buying reviews, filtering the list down to the customers you know are delighted, attaching a discount to the request: all three get reviews deleted, and a pattern of them does worse than that. Anybody can generate asks. The asks are worth nothing if the account they land on gets flagged.

So here is exactly what the Review Engine does, in order.

## The three questions every owner asks

**Will this put me on the wrong side of Google?** No, and that is the whole design. It is built to stay inside Google's rules, which is also the only version of it that keeps working after month three. You get the one-page summary so you are never taking our word for it.

**How many reviews will we get?** We will not promise you a number, and be careful with anyone who does. What we commit to is that every past customer gets asked, the same way, every time. How many of them write something is up to them.

**Can we offer a discount in exchange for a review?** No. Google bans paying or rewarding for reviews and deletes them when it catches it, which means the discount costs you money and buys you nothing. It goes on the win-back offer instead, where the customer gets a real reason to come back and the review stays clean.

## The customers who went quiet are not lost

A customer who stopped calling looks exactly like a customer you lost. In most files they are the same row: a name and a date. There is no column that says *she was happy, she simply moved on*, or *the last job went wrong and nobody found out*. So the quiet half of the list never gets worked, because nobody can tell which names are worth a call. That is the same rot as [a CRM database nobody dares to clean up](https://birkystudio.com/blog/crm-database-rot-what-to-keep/), pointed at customers instead of contacts.

The win-back offer is how we find out which quiet customers are actually gone. Most of them are not gone. They had no reason to come back and nobody gave them one.

## Why the ask has to be specific

There is one more reason requests fail, and it is the reason most of them read as spam even when they come from a real business: there is nothing in them. *Just checking in, hope all is well.* A customer cannot remember what they bought, when, or from whom, and neither can your file, so the message has no choice but to be empty.

That is the same failure mode as [a follow-up with nothing in it](https://birkystudio.com/blog/why-your-follow-ups-get-ignored/), and the fix is the same one. A record is what makes a message specific, and a specific message is the difference between a tap and a delete. If you want the fundamentals of asking, [How to Get More Customer Reviews](https://birkystudio.com/blog/how-to-get-more-customer-reviews-online/) covers the ground most businesses have not done yet.

## What it will not do

- **It will not write a word of a review.** We ask, the customer writes. Anything else is fake, and fake is what Google removes.
- **It will not only ask the happy ones.** Filtering the ask down to customers you know are thrilled breaks Google's rules and produces a page that reads perfectly while converting like a brochure nobody believes.
- **It cannot promise a rating.** Nobody can. Your rating moves as your customers move, and a number quoted to you in advance is a marketing line, not a forecast.
- **Text requests need documented consent first.** Where you have it, we use it. Where you do not, email is the default rather than something we do anyway.
- **It needs a Google Business Profile to exist first.** If the profile itself is thin, unverified or missing photos, that is groundwork to sort out before the review ask, rather than something this does for you.
