There's a thread on r/RealEstateTechnology that deserves more attention than it got. An agent pointed out something uncomfortable: *wire fraud barely comes up* in real estate tech conversations, *despite losses running into the hundreds of millions annually.*

Read that gap again. Hundreds of millions of dollars a year, and the topic is basically a footnote.

While everyone argues about lead response times and CRM features, the single most expensive thing that can happen on a transaction — a buyer wiring their down payment into a criminal's account — sits in the "somebody else's problem" pile at most brokerages.

I don't think it's because agents don't care. I think it's because nobody has *time* to build a process around it. And that's exactly the kind of gap that costs you a deal, a client, or a reputation.

## How the Scam Actually Works

It's not sophisticated. That's the scary part.

A fraudster gets into — or convincingly spoofs — the email thread between the buyer, the agent, and the title company. Usually right when everyone is tired and moving fast: final week before closing, inspection done, appraisal back, everyone's focused on the finish line.

Then an email arrives that looks like it's from the title company. New wiring instructions. Slightly different account. Urgent tone.

The buyer, who has never wired this much money in their life, follows the instructions. Poof. Six figures, gone. And once it's wired, it's usually gone for good — the money bounces through accounts before anyone realizes the closing table email was fake.

The cruel detail: the buyer often *trusts* it precisely because they think their agent is managing the process. Which means when it goes wrong, the relationship — and the referral — goes with it.

## Why Agents Are Exposed (Own It, Then Fix It)

Here's the honest part. The reason this works is that transaction communication is *scattered*.

The lender calls your cell with a rate change. The inspector texts you a finding. Title emails a date change. The buyer WhatsApps a question at 9pm. Every one of those is a thread, a channel, and a place where the real conversation and the fake one can blend together.

And when a fraudster inserts themselves into that mess, there is no single record to compare against. No "here's everything that was actually said on this transaction." Just a dozen inboxes and a gut feeling.

This is the same fragmentation problem agents describe constantly — the one where every new tool starts over from scratch at the address level and *the agent becomes the integration* between systems that don't talk.

Wire fraud isn't a different problem. It's the worst-case version of the same one.

## The Low-Tech Fix That Actually Stops It

Security people will tell you the same thing: the defense is embarrassingly simple.

**Verbal confirmation of wiring instructions over a phone number you already know.** Not a number in the email. A number from your existing contact card, or better, one you and the title officer agree on *at the start of the deal*, before any money is in motion.

Every wire instruction change = a live phone call. No exceptions, no "just this once," no matter how legitimate the email looks.

That single rule defeats the overwhelming majority of these attacks, because the fraudster can copy an email signature but they can't answer the phone on the title company's real line.

The problem isn't that agents don't know this. It's that the rule only works if it's *enforced* — every transaction, every time, even when it's 5pm on a Friday and everyone wants to go home.
