The colors you choose for your business website do more than make it look nice. They shape how visitors feel about your brand, how long they stay on your pages, and whether they trust you enough to buy. Research shows that color increases brand recognition by up to 80%, and 90% of snap judgments about products are based on color alone.

Yet most small business owners pick colors based on personal preference rather than strategy. Here is how to choose website colors that actually work for your business.

## The 60-30-10 Rule

Professional designers use a simple formula for balancing colors on a website. The 60-30-10 rule works like this:

- **60% dominant color.** This is your background. Most business websites use white, light gray, or a very soft brand color. It creates space and lets your content breathe.
- **30% secondary color.** This appears on section backgrounds, cards, and sidebars. It adds visual interest without overwhelming the page.
- **10% accent color.** This is for buttons, links, and calls to action. It should be your boldest, most attention-grabbing color because it guides visitors toward the actions you want them to take.

This ratio prevents your website from looking chaotic while still giving it personality.

## Colors and What They Communicate

Every color carries psychological associations. Understanding these helps you pick colors that support your business message rather than work against it.

**Blue** conveys trust, professionalism, and reliability. This is why banks, tech companies, and healthcare providers use it so heavily. If your business depends on customer trust, blue is a safe choice.

**Green** suggests growth, health, and nature. It works well for wellness brands, organic products, financial services, and environmental companies.

**Red** creates urgency and excitement. It draws attention fast, which is why sale tags and clearance signs are red. Use it sparingly on websites because too much red feels aggressive.

**Orange** is friendly and energetic. It works well for calls to action because it is noticeable without being aggressive. Many successful "Buy Now" and "Sign Up" buttons are orange.

**Black and dark gray** communicate luxury, sophistication, and authority. High-end brands, law firms, and creative agencies often use dark color schemes.

**Purple** suggests creativity, wisdom, and premium quality. It is popular with beauty brands, creative agencies, and educational services.

The key is matching your colors to the feeling you want customers to have when they visit your site. A children's daycare should not use the same color scheme as a law firm.

## Contrast and Readability Matter Most

You can pick the perfect brand colors and still have a terrible website if people cannot read your text. Contrast is the difference between your text color and your background color.

**The basics:**
- Dark text on light backgrounds is the easiest to read
- Avoid light gray text on white backgrounds
- Avoid placing text over busy images without a dark overlay
- Body text should be at least 16 pixels tall

**Test your colors.** Use a free tool like the WebAIM Contrast Checker (webaim.org/resources/contrastchecker) to verify your text and background combinations meet accessibility standards. A failing contrast score means some visitors literally cannot read your content.

## The Accent Color Strategy

Your accent color is the most important choice on your entire website. It goes on buttons, links, and calls to action. If visitors cannot easily spot where to click, they will not click.

**Rules for accent colors:**
- Pick a color that stands out from everything else on the page
- Use it ONLY for interactive elements (buttons, links, form fields)
- Never use your accent color for large background areas
- Make sure it has enough contrast against both light and dark backgrounds

A common mistake is using the same blue for text links, section backgrounds, and buttons. When everything is the same color, nothing stands out. Your accent color should feel like a spotlight pointing at the most important action on each page.

## How Many Colors Is Too Many?

The short answer: three to four. Your website needs:

1. A dominant background color (usually white or off-white)
2. A secondary color for sections and cards
3. A primary brand color for headings and key elements
4. An accent color for buttons and calls to action

More than four colors creates visual noise. Visitors feel overwhelmed and leave. If you already have a logo with multiple colors, pick the two strongest ones and build your website palette around them. The other logo colors can appear in the logo itself without spreading across every page.

## Real Examples That Work

**Restaurants and food businesses** do well with warm colors (red, orange, yellow) paired with dark backgrounds. These colors stimulate appetite and create a welcoming atmosphere.

**Professional services** (accountants, lawyers, consultants) benefit from blue or dark color schemes with clean white space. The message is competence and reliability.

**Health and wellness** businesses thrive with greens and soft blues. These colors feel calming and natural, which matches the customer's desired experience.

**Creative agencies and designers** can push boundaries with bold color combinations, but the layout still needs structure. Even the most creative site needs readable text and clear navigation.

## What to Do Right Now

If you already have a website, look at it with fresh eyes. Can visitors immediately spot your call-to-action buttons? Is your text easy to read? Do your colors feel intentional or random?

If you are building a new site, start with the 60-30-10 rule. Pick your background color first, then your secondary color, then your accent. Test the combination on a real screen before committing.
