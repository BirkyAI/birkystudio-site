Every few months, someone in r/RealEstateTechnology posts a version of the same question: *"I need some advice for cleaning up my database."* The most recent one came from an agent with about 140 transactions on the books, a decade of contacts, and no idea which of those records were still people.

The replies were more interesting than the question. It split three ways, and the split is the real story:

- **The privacy camp:** there is nothing your clients will like better than you handing all their contact info and transaction notes to an AI company's database.
- **The AI camp:** this is exactly what AI is good for, as long as you actually have a CRM.
- **The purist camp:** spend a day and do the work yourself, not every problem needs a tech solution.

All three are partly right, which is usually a sign that everyone is arguing about the wrong thing. The cleanup is not the problem. The pile is.

## The cleanup is not the problem, the pile is

Nobody sets out to build a database full of dead numbers. What actually happens is smaller and more boring. A lead comes in during a busy week, gets a name and a phone number, and nothing else. No note, no context, no next step. It sits there. Six months later you cannot remember whether this person was a buyer, a seller, a referral, or somebody's cousin.

Multiply that by four hundred records and you get a mass cleanup you keep postponing, because doing it properly means reliving four hundred half memories in one weekend.

The pile is not evidence that you were disorganized. It is evidence that nothing ever gave each record a next step, so nothing ever came out of it. That distinction matters, because it decides what you should do next: not a heroic weekend of deleting, but a rule that stops the pile from re-forming.

## The four-question filter

This is the part people actually need, so here it is as a rule you can run tonight. Take one record. Ask four questions, in this order. Stop as soon as you have an answer.

**1. Did money move between you in the last 24 months?**
Yes means past client, and past clients are not a cleanup problem, they are a referral asset. They go to their own pile and get their own cadence. Do not merge them into the prospect list and do not let them sit among strangers.

**2. Have they replied to anything you sent in the last 12 months?**
A reply is the only real signal of life. Opens are noise. A one word answer, a thumbs up, a question back, all count. If they replied, they are live, and live records need a next step this week.

**3. Would you recognize the name on a caller ID?**
This one stings the first hundred times. If the honest answer is no, and question one is also no, then what you are holding is a row, not a person. The information that would make it a person was written on a sticky note in 2021 and is gone.

**4. Is there a next step you would genuinely do in the next 30 days?**
Not a step you could do. A step you would do. If the answer is no, the record does not belong in your working list, and it does not need to be deleted either.

That produces four piles, and only one of them is a delete pile, which brings me to the part most cleanup advice skips.

## Nothing gets deleted today

The instinct when you finally attack the pile is to purge it. Do not. Archive it instead.

Deleting feels decisive and it is usually a mistake, because the value of an old record is not in the field you can see, it is in the memory that comes back when you read the name. I have watched agents delete nine hundred contacts on a Saturday and then spend a week trying to remember who referred the client that closed in March.

Archiving costs nothing and it is reversible. Move the dead pile out of your working view, keep it exportable, and move on. If it is genuinely dead, you will never open it again. If you were wrong about one, you have not lost anything that mattered.

## The person who did 2,500 by hand

The best reply in that thread came from an agent who cleaned 2,500 leads over six months by hand, and framed it as three stages: clean up, set up, level up. Merge the duplicates, delete what you will never work, give every kept contact a next step, and then treat the ongoing upkeep as daily prospecting rather than a quarterly crisis.

That is the correct answer, and I want to be clear that it is the correct answer even if you hire somebody. The reason the pile comes back is never the cleanup. It is that the setup stage, the part where every record gets a next step and a cadence, is the part nobody does, because it is one small action per contact, forever.

## Why the fields stay empty

There is a version of this problem that looks identical and is actually different. You bought a CRM with fields for everything: source, budget, timeline, motivation, last contact. Two years later the fields are mostly blank.

That is not a discipline failure either. It is what happens when logging takes longer than the conversation did. I wrote about that side of it in [why your CRM is making you work harder](/blog/crm-is-making-you-work-harder/), and the short version is that a field you have to remember to fill is a field that will be empty within a month.

The cleanup and the empty fields are the same disease: the record only gets written when someone has spare time, and nobody has spare time.

## What it will not do

The agent will not decide who is worth keeping. That is a business judgment about your own relationships and it belongs to you, which is why the four questions above are questions rather than an automatic score.

It will not repair a phone number that was typed wrong in 2019. If the correct number exists nowhere in your stack, no software can invent it.

It will not delete anything on its own. It archives, flags and reports, and a human confirms.

And it will not hand you a "we removed 38% of your duplicates" number, because your duplicate rate depends on how your database was built, not on us. Anybody quoting a figure like that measured their own book or made it up.
