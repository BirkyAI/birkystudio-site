> *"I should not need 8 tabs and a spreadsheet just to answer one property question."*, r/RealEstateTechnology, 2026

That line was the most repeated complaint in the whole thread, and it is the one I hear most often in real life too. For years I read it as a software complaint. Buy better tools, connect them, done.

Then I spent a week following one number instead of a client, and I stopped reading it that way.

*What follows is a composite. It is not one deal and not one client, but every step in it is something I have watched happen, more than once, in an ordinary week.*

## Eight tabs is not a habit problem

Nobody opens eight tabs for fun. Each tab gets opened because closing it means trusting a copy of a number you did not check yourself.

So I followed one number. It was an HOA fee, and it had four homes.

## Where the number was born

March. The listing agreement, page three. The seller writes $295 a month in the fee field and initials it. That is the original. It lives as a scan attached to an email, where nobody will ever look at it again unless something goes wrong.

## First copy

April 2. The listing agent types $295 into the MLS field. Now there are two copies, and only one of them was typed by a person who was looking at the signed page.

## Second copy

April 9. I type it into my own CRM note after a showing, reading it off the MLS screen, on my phone, standing in a driveway. This one is a copy of a copy, and nothing is wrong yet. This is the step where I usually tell myself I will verify it later.

## Third copy

April 11. I send it to the buyer on WhatsApp: "HOA is $295 a month." Four places now hold this number, and it is sitting in a message thread with a client in it, which is the most dangerous place a number can live, because it is the place a client will quote back to you.

Four homes for one line of text. Two of them were typed by people who never saw the original.

## The change nobody wrote down

June. The board raises the dues to $310. The property manager tells the listing agent on a phone call. The listing agent tells nobody in writing. The MLS keeps $295, because nobody logged in to change it. The agreement still says $295, because nobody re-signed anything.

Read that part again, because it took me years to see it. After June, the correct number is $310, and there is no document anywhere that says $310. The only record of the real number is a phone call in June and the memory of two people.

## The day it bit

September. My buyer's lender pulls the fee off the MLS to finish the payment sheet. It does not match what my client has been telling her own bank since April. Now three versions of the truth are moving through one closing: the $310 the board charges, the $295 in the MLS and the listing agreement, and the $295 I typed in a driveway.

Nobody in that chain did anything malicious. That is exactly what makes this durable. Every one of them did the reasonable thing, and the number still went wrong.

## So what are the tabs, really

They are not a discipline problem. Nobody is lazy. And they are not really a software problem either, though that is the easier thing to blame.

Four systems were willing to hold that number. Not one of them owned it. When nobody owns a fact, every professional who touches it keeps a private copy, and keeps all the private copies open, permanently, because closing a tab means trusting somebody else's version. Multiply that by the dozens of facts inside one active deal and you arrive at eight tabs. It is not a browsing habit. It is a stack of small unresolved ownership questions, left open for months at a time.

The same shape turns up in market data. Three tools will hand you a comp set for the same house, and [the real cost of one comps check](/blog/market-data-comps-on-demand/) is that you keep all three open because none of them is the authority. It turns up at the address level too, where [duplicate property records](/blog/duplicate-property-records-cost-you-deals/) mean the same house exists three times and the software has no idea.

## What actually closes the tabs

Three moves, and none of them is buying another dashboard.

**Name an owner for each fact, once, and write it down.** The signed agreement owns money and dates. The MLS owns the listing facts. Your CRM owns what the client actually said. Your calendar owns timing. Anything that moves by phone gets a note within the hour, or it is not a record, it is a rumor. What matters is not the list. What matters is that you decide it once, instead of re-deciding it every time a question arrives.

**Keep the link to the source, not just the value.** $295 in a CRM note with no provenance is a number with no address. $295 with "MLS field, read April 9" at least tells you how stale it is when September arrives.

## What it will not do

- It cannot repair a source that was never written. If the only record of $310 is a June phone call, no software will find it. What it can do is tell you that the number you are holding was last verified in April, which is the sentence that stops you quoting it in September.
- It cannot see a system you do not connect. An unconnected lender portal stays a tab.
- It will ask, not decide, when two copies disagree. Two numbers disagreeing is a question for a human, and a tool that silently picks one is worse than no tool.
- It will not invent a savings figure for you. I am not going to quote you an hours-saved number. How many copies of one fact your county, your MLS and your office each keep is different in every market. Anyone who gives you a single figure for all of them made it up.
