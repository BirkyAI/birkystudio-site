> "Biggest drop-off isn't lead capture, it's the gap between 'someone showed interest' and 'someone is actually booked in'"

An agent posted that line in r/RealEstateTechnology, and it has been stuck in my head since, because it drags the problem away from the top of the funnel. Not lead volume. Not ad spend. The quiet middle, where people have already raised a hand and are waiting to hear from somebody.

Here is the part that line leaves out, though. The follow-up does get sent. Often. A message goes out, the phone stays quiet, and the agent files the name under cold and moves on to the next one.

Most of the time the name was not cold. The message was.

## Two emails to the same person

*Both drafts below are composites. The names and the details are illustrative. The difference between them is not.*

```
Subject: Following up

Hi Mark,

Just following up on our conversation from the weekend. Let me know if you
would like to see anything else, happy to help.

Thanks,
```

```
Subject: The Mill Street roof, and your school-year window

Hi Mark,

Two things from Saturday I do not want to lose.

You compared Cedar Ridge against the house on Mill Street, and the thing
that decided it for you was the roof, because of the question you asked
about what it costs to replace one. Mill Street was re-roofed in 2021, so
that question is off the table.

You also mentioned wanting to be moved before the school year starts.
That gives us a window and I would rather not waste it.

One question only you can answer: if the roof is no longer a concern, what
is the other thing holding you back from a second look?

[You]
```

Both emails are short. Both are polite. Neither one pressures anybody into anything.

The second one carries the two addresses Mark compared, the question he asked in the driveway, the answer to that question, and the deadline he mentioned in passing. The first one says "I am still here". The second one says "I heard you".

## Where those details went

The reason most agents send the first email is not laziness and it is not bad manners. It is that the second email cannot be written on Tuesday evening, because its raw material was standing in a driveway on Saturday, and by Tuesday evening all you have is a name and a vague sense that it went well.

I have written about this from the client's side before, in [Clients Remember What You Forget](/blog/clients-remember-what-you-forget/). The part nobody says out loud is the one that costs money. The memory that matters is not whether you remember the person. It is whether you remember the four specific facts that make a message worth answering.

## Four things a reply can be

Every follow-up you send comes back as one of four things:

- **A yes.** There is a next step, and it is in the calendar.
- **A date.** "After the holidays" is not a no, it is a follow-up you will never send, and [what a missed follow-up really costs](/blog/cost-of-missed-follow-up/) is usually paid a season later, quietly.
- **A real no.** Rare, useful, worth having, because it lets you stop.
- **Silence.**

Three of those four are information. Only the last one gets read as a verdict, and it is the one that tells you the least. Before you believe it, read what you sent. If there is no question in it, no detail in it, and nothing that only that person could answer, you were not ignored. You got no reply, which is a different thing.
